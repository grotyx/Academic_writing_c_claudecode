# Introduction

## Moves
1. Territory (one paragraph, 3-5 sentences). The clinical problem, its burden and current
   management. Present tense for established facts; every factual sentence cited.
2. Gap (one paragraph). What remains unknown, conflicting or weakly supported, named precisely:
   population, comparator, outcome, design, follow-up. Not "few studies exist".
3. Aim (one short paragraph, 1-3 sentences). The objective and, when prespecified, the hypothesis.
Length: 3-4 paragraphs, about 300-500 words. No results, no methods detail, no narrative review.

## Rules
- Open on the clinical problem, not on history ("Since 1934...") or a generic truism ("X is a
  common disease worldwide").
- The gap follows from the territory paragraph and names the limitation of prior evidence
  (small samples, retrospective designs, short follow-up, surrogate outcomes, single centre).
- End on the aim. Do not preview findings; claim priority only when the draft plan verifies it.

## Phrasebank
- Territory: "X affects approximately ... of ...", "X remains the standard treatment for ...",
  "Current guidelines recommend ...", "Y has been proposed as an alternative to X because ..."
- Gap: "However, comparisons of X with Y are limited to ...", "Previous studies were small,
  retrospective, or ...", "Whether X ... remains uncertain.", "The effect of X on Y has not been
  evaluated in ..."
- Aim: "This study aimed to compare ...", "We therefore evaluated whether ...", "We hypothesized
  that X would be noninferior to Y with respect to ..."

## Avoid -> prefer
- "In recent years, there has been growing interest in X" -> say what X is and why it matters.
- "X plays a crucial role in Y" -> "X determines Y" or "X is associated with Y", with a citation.
- "Few studies have examined ..." -> "Two retrospective series of fewer than 100 patients ..."
- "To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study" -> describe the specific gap instead.

## Model paragraphs
Lumbar spinal stenosis is the most frequent indication for spine surgery in adults older than 65
years [EVID:author_year]. Decompression without fusion relieves leg pain in most patients, but up
to one in five undergo reoperation within 5 years, most often for recurrent stenosis or
instability [EVID:author_year]. Endoscopic decompression was developed to limit damage to the
posterior elements and could therefore reduce the risk of postoperative instability
[EVID:author_year].

Evidence for this premise remains limited. Published comparisons of endoscopic and microscopic
decompression enrolled fewer than 150 patients, followed them for 2 years or less, and reported
pain scores rather than reoperation [EVID:author_year]. Whether the smaller exposure translates
into fewer reoperations is unknown.

This study aimed to compare the 5-year reoperation rate after endoscopic and microscopic
decompression for lumbar spinal stenosis. We hypothesized that endoscopic decompression would be
associated with fewer reoperations for instability.
